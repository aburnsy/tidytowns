# Prehistoric Two Mile Borris

*5500 BC – AD 450*

<div class="heritage-hero" markdown="1">
The deep prehistory of this place is wetland country. The River Suir and its tributaries running south-west, the Slieveardagh hills west of that, and to the north and east the great Derryville and Littleton bogs. In prehistoric times the bogs were larger, wetter, and pretty much impassable. They were also full of food. Hunting, fowling and fishing happened in the wet places. So did ritual deposits. An Iron Age shield made of wood and leather was pulled out of a bog at Clonoura, and a possible female bog body was found at Newhill. Both of those are within 5km of the village.
</div>

The prehistoric site dug along the M8 corridor in 2007–2009 sits on slightly higher ground, on a low glacial ridge with the land falling gently towards the Black River to the west. People used this ridge for over a thousand years. They lived on the high ground. They buried and cremated their dead near the crest. And on low ground beside the river they built a large enclosure for something that was clearly more than just everyday life.

## Enclosure D, the ritual enclosure

<div class="heritage-finding" markdown="1">
<h3>The big oval enclosure beside the river</h3>
<div class="finding-meta">Late Bronze Age, animal bone in burnt ditch fill radiocarbon-dated 800–510 BC</div>

A Late Bronze Age ritual enclosure was discovered on low ground at the meeting of the Black River and a small unnamed stream. It was oval in plan, roughly 95 metres east-to-west by 80 metres north-to-south. The entrance faced east-south-east, was 3.8 metres wide, and was framed by a pair of large post-holes. The ditch was wider and deeper near the entrance than anywhere else, which is the kind of thing people do when they want the entrance to *feel* important.

The ditch fills near the entrance were waterlogged when excavated, and a fragment of human skull was recovered from the northern terminus. Elsewhere along the ditch, low-intensity in-situ burning was visible, with charcoal and heat-shattered stone in varying concentrations. Whether the whole ditch was set alight as one big event, or fires were lit in lots of small ones across many years, isn't yet clear. A flint scraper was found in the burnt layer.
</div>

??? note "About the cattle burial"

    Inside the enclosure, on the raised central area, sat a cluster of pits and post-holes that are not thought to be a house. The upper fills of the central group were charcoal-rich. Two cremation burials were found in the interior. So was a single, deliberate cattle burial.

    The cattle burial is the find that stops you in your tracks. The selected bones of a mature animal, around twelve years old, had been placed in three or four overlapping, criss-crossing layers in a small shallow pit about two-thirds of a metre across. Three small wooden stakes had been driven into the ground first, then the bones were arranged on top.

    Cattle bone shows up on prehistoric sites all the time, but a deliberate, structured deposition like this inside a ritual enclosure is rare. Ó Droma points out that maintaining an animal to old age, twelve is old for a cow, is a "tacit acknowledgement of an additional sentimental, symbolic or cultural value beyond economic usefulness". His comparison was with the holy cows of Asia. Whatever this animal meant to the people who buried her, she was clearly someone.

## The circular house

<div class="heritage-finding" markdown="1">
<h3>A roundhouse on the ridge</h3>
<div class="finding-meta">Probable Late Bronze Age, similar size to dated examples nearby</div>

Up on higher ground, the dig found a circular house 6.8 metres in diameter, defined by a curving slot-trench, internal post-holes, and an annex or porch on the eastern side. Later ploughing had removed the floor levels, but enough of the foundation cuts survived to show the form. A nearly identical Bronze Age house at Killoran, about 10km north, dates to the early to mid second millennium BC.

The excavation team's preferred description of the size: a roundhouse this big could "comfortably accommodate seven people (and a dog)".
</div>

## Burial monuments and a flat cemetery

Three more burial monuments turned up on the dig, mostly on the crest and west side of the central ridge:

- **Two ring-ditches** (circular ditches enclosing nothing, just small amounts of cremated bone in the ditch fills)
- **A rectangular ditched monument** 4 metres long and 2.2 metres wide, with a central cremation pit. Rectangular ditches of this funerary type are unusual, possibly unique, in the Irish record.
- **A flat cemetery** of 18 cremation burials, scattered without a covering monument.

Cremated bone, the careful enclosing ditches, and the flat-cemetery scatter together suggest several centuries of repeated burial activity in the same patch of high ground. People kept coming back here to deal with their dead.

## What this tells us

The picture from prehistoric Two Mile Borris is of a community that was farming the higher ground, maintaining a separate ritual area on the low ground by the river, and treating one elderly cow with enough reverence to bury her like a person. The ritual enclosure was used for centuries. The wetlands all around were treated as places where things, and probably people, were given to the water on purpose.

It is a long way from the village we have now, but the people on this ground 3,000 years ago picked the same patch of ground at the meeting of the river and the small stream, and kept choosing it.
