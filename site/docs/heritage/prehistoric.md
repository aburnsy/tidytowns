# Prehistoric Two Mile Borris

*5500 BC – AD 450*

<div class="heritage-hero" markdown="1">
The deep prehistory of this place is wetland country. The River Suir and its tributaries running south-west, the Slieveardagh hills west of that, and to the north and east the great Derryville and Littleton bogs. In prehistoric times the bogs were larger, wetter, and pretty much impassable. They were also full of food. Hunting, fowling and fishing happened in the wet places. So did ritual deposits. An Iron Age shield made of wood and leather was pulled out of a bog at Clonoura, and a possible female bog body was found at Newhill. Both of those are within 5km of the village.
</div>

The picture of prehistoric Two Mile Borris that we have today comes mainly from a series of seven archaeological excavations along the line of the M8/N8 motorway south and east of the village, dug in 2006 and 2007 by Valerie J. Keeley Ltd before construction began. The sites sit on slightly higher ground, with the land falling gently away to the Black River and the bogs. People used this corner of Tipperary for over a thousand years of the Bronze Age alone, and they used it in distinctly different ways at different places.

## Enclosure D, the ritual enclosure (AR 33)

![Aerial view of Enclosure D from Site AR 33, looking west](../assets/map-data/photos/dig_ar33/plate04-enclosure-d-aerial.jpg)
*Aerial view of Enclosure D, AR 33, with the south-eastern entrance visible. Photo: Airshots Ltd / Valerie J. Keeley Ltd / TII (placeholder).*

<div class="heritage-finding" markdown="1">
<h3>The big oval enclosure beside the river</h3>
<div class="finding-meta">Late Bronze Age; ditch fills radiocarbon-dated 800-510 BC</div>

A Late Bronze Age oval enclosure was discovered at Site AR 33, on the east-facing slope above the Black River. It was 95 metres east-to-west, with a 3.8 metre wide entrance facing south-east, framed by a pair of large post-holes. The ditch was wider and deeper near the entrance than anywhere else, which is the kind of thing people do when they want the entrance to feel important.

The ditch fills near the entrance were waterlogged when excavated, and a fragment of human skull was recovered from the northern terminus of the south-eastern ditch. Elsewhere along the ditch, low-intensity in-situ burning was visible, with charcoal and heat-shattered stone in varying concentrations. Whether the whole ditch was set alight as one big event, or fires were lit in lots of small ones across many years, isn't yet clear. A flint scraper was found in the burnt layer, and 90 kilograms of animal bone came out of the ditch fills.
</div>

??? note "About the cattle burial"

    Inside the enclosure, on the raised central area, sat a cluster of pits and post-holes that are not thought to be a house. The upper fills of the central group were charcoal-rich. The find that stops you in your tracks, though, is the single, deliberate cattle burial.

    The selected bones of a mature animal, around twelve years old, had been placed in three or four overlapping, criss-crossing layers in a small shallow pit about two-thirds of a metre across. Three small wooden stakes had been driven into the ground first, then the bones were arranged on top.

    Cattle bone shows up on prehistoric sites all the time, but a deliberate, structured deposition like this inside a ritual enclosure is rare. The excavation team's read is that maintaining an animal to old age, twelve is old for a Bronze Age cow, is a tacit acknowledgement of an additional sentimental, symbolic or cultural value beyond economic usefulness. Whatever this animal meant to the people who buried her, she was clearly someone.

## The Middle Bronze Age roundhouse (AR 31)

<div class="heritage-finding" markdown="1">
<h3>A roundhouse for seven people and a dog</h3>
<div class="finding-meta">Middle Bronze Age; radiocarbon-dated 1607-1451 cal BC</div>

Up on the higher ground at the western end of the dig, where the line of the new N75 link road crossed the small ridge in Borris townland, the dig found a wattle-and-timber roundhouse 6.8 metres in diameter, with an annex or porch on its east side and a south-east facing entrance. Later ploughing had removed the floor levels, but the foundation slot trench and 21 internal post-holes and 70 stake-holes survived. There was no evidence of a hearth.

The excavation team's preferred description of the size: a roundhouse this big could "comfortably accommodate seven people (and a dog)". A nearly identical Middle Bronze Age house at Killoran 8, about 10 km north of here, has been dated to the same era.
</div>

In one of the wider Borris townland fields, well above the floodplain, this is where everyday Bronze Age life was happening at the time when the cattle burial was being made up the road at Enclosure D.

## The flat cremation cemeteries (AR 32 and AR 36)

![Cremation pit [c5] at AR 32](../assets/map-data/photos/dig_ar32/plate05-cremation-pit-c5.jpg)
*Cremation pit [c5] at AR 32, dated 1603-1427 cal BC, with a large burnt-bone fragment in section. Scale 0.40m. Photo: Valerie J. Keeley Ltd / TII (placeholder).*

The dead were dealt with at two distinct flat cremation cemeteries in the Bronze Age, on what is now plough ground south and south-east of the village.

The earlier cemetery was at [AR 36](../places/dig_ar36.md), about a kilometre south-east of AR 32, where 13 pits contained the cremated remains of 13 individuals, with a further 5 pits interpreted as cenotaphs or "blind" burials. Two radiocarbon dates put the burials between 2204-2038 and 2024-1896 cal BC, an Early Bronze Age range that overlaps with the burnt-mound trough at AR 35 nearby.

The later cemetery was at [AR 32](../places/dig_ar32.md), a tight 12 by 15 metre cluster of 9 cremation pits and 11 "blind" cenotaph pits, dating to the Middle Bronze Age (radiocarbon dates 1619-1454 cal BC, 1603-1427 cal BC and 1492-1322 cal BC). The remarkable thing about the layout is that, except for one case, none of the pits cut into each other. That tells you the cemetery was clearly marked above ground, probably with stakes, small stones or wooden posts. The upper fill of one of the pits actually held a posthole, which seals it.

Cremated bone, the careful enclosing pits, and the way people kept coming back to the same patches of ground, suggest several centuries of repeated burial activity. People had ideas about where the dead belonged, and they stuck to them.

## The burnt mounds (AR 35 and AR 37)

A burnt mound, "fulacht fia" in Irish, is one of the most common prehistoric site types you find in the Irish landscape. The basic recipe is a sunken wooden trough, a fireplace alongside it to heat large stones red-hot, and a low horseshoe-shaped mound of cracked, fire-shattered stones that builds up next to the trough over years of repeated use. What exactly people were using the troughs for is still debated: cooking, brewing, washing wool, dyeing, and ritual bathing have all been proposed. Whatever they were, they were popular and they were everywhere from roughly 2500 BC to 1000 BC.

Two adjacent burnt-mound sites turned up on the same low rise on the M8 alignment. [AR 35](../places/dig_ar35.md) had two sub-areas about 30 metres apart, with troughs dating to the late Neolithic / Early Bronze Age transition (2486-2299 cal BC) and to the Middle Bronze Age (1880-1660 cal BC). [AR 37](../places/dig_ar37.md) added three more groups of features, including a Middle Bronze Age trough (1740-1520 cal BC) and a trough under a separate burnt mound (1367-1129 cal BC).

## What this tells us

A picture of more or less unbroken use across about 1,500 years of the Bronze Age. People were living and farming on the higher ground in Middle Bronze Age timber roundhouses, doing ritual or industrial work involving boiling water and hot stones down at the burnt mounds, burying their cremated dead in carefully maintained flat cemeteries on the south-facing slopes, and at some point in the Late Bronze Age building a 95-metre enclosure on low ground beside the river and laying a venerable old cow to rest inside it. The wetlands all around were treated as places where things, and probably people, were given to the water on purpose.

It is a long way from the village we have now, but the people on this ground 3,000 years ago picked the same patch of ground at the meeting of the river and the small stream, and kept choosing it.

## Where on the ground

None of these features are visible at the surface today. Most were excavated, recorded and then built over by the M8/N8 corridor or covered with topsoil and returned to tillage. There are no SMR/RMP records for them as upstanding monuments. The exception is the wider landscape: the Iron Age shield from Clonoura and the bog body from Newhill, both within 5 km of the village, are in museum collections.
