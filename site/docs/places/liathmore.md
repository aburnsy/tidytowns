Liathmore is the monastic site about 2.8km east of the village, in the townland of Leigh, and it is one of the more remarkable bits of heritage in the parish. It was founded by Saint Mochoemog, who according to tradition established the monastery here in the 6th century and died in 655 AD. Mochoemog is also the saint our village national school, Scoil Mochaomhóg Naofa NS, is named after, and the school's annual feast day on 13th March is the day the original school opened in 1846. So the line connecting Liathmore to the village runs unbroken from the 6th century right up to the present day, which is one of the loveliest things about the place.

What is left on the ground today is two ruined churches and the foundation circle of an ancient round tower, all sitting together as a single complex. The smaller of the two churches is the earlier, dating to the early medieval period. The larger church is later: begun in the 12th century, with most of the structure dating to the 15th, and it contains a number of tombs. The headline feature on the larger church is a Romanesque doorway in beautifully carved stone, weathered by centuries of Tipperary rain but still very much there for those who know where to look.

Tucked into the doorway is the Liathmore sheela-na-gig, a small medieval carved figure dated to the 12th century. She is lying on her left side with her triangular head and large eyes pointing toward the inside of the church, and the carving is delicate enough that heavy lichen and the weather have taken some of the detail. The sheela-na-gig and the Romanesque carving are both protected as part of the National Monument designation for the site.

The connection between Liathmore and Two Mile Borris also runs through the village name. Our older Irish name "Borrisleigh" almost certainly relates to the monastic community of Liathmore-Mochoemóg in nearby Leigh townland, so the village we live in today carries the saint's monastery in its very name.

The early medieval cemetery uncovered south of the village during the 2007–2009 dig contained 20 east-west aligned burials right beside a working ringfort, and Liathmore is plausibly where the community's burial practice moved next, on Ó Droma's reading, once burial in consecrated ecclesiastical ground became the norm. The line that runs from those small graves to the visible Christian cemetery here at Liathmore is plausibly the same community of people. For more on that connection, see [Early Medieval Two Mile Borris](../heritage/early-medieval.md) in our Heritage section.
