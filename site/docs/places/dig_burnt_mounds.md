A pair of adjacent excavations on the same low rise on the M8 alignment, both of them Bronze Age burnt mound sites. AR 35 and AR 37 sat roughly thirty metres apart and were excavated by Gary Conboy in October and November 2006.

A burnt mound, "fulacht fia" in Irish, is one of the most common prehistoric site types you find in the Irish landscape. The basic recipe is a sunken wooden trough, a fireplace alongside it to heat large stones red-hot, and a low horseshoe-shaped mound of cracked, fire-shattered stones that builds up next to the trough over years of repeated use. What exactly people were using the troughs for is still debated by archaeologists: cooking, brewing, washing wool, dyeing, and ritual bathing have all been proposed. Whatever they were, they were popular and they were everywhere, from roughly 2500 BC to 1000 BC.

AR 35 produced two sub-sites about thirty metres apart. The earlier one (Site B) had two troughs and five pits, with one trough dated to 2486-2299 cal BC, right at the late Neolithic to Early Bronze Age transition. The later one (Site A) was a classic Middle Bronze Age burnt mound with a single irregular trough underneath; timbers from the trough dated to 1880-1660 cal BC. AR 37 added three more feature groups on the same patch: an unaccompanied Middle Bronze Age trough dated 1740-1520 cal BC, a trough under a separate burnt mound dated 1367-1129 cal BC, and two isolated pit clusters.

For more on what people were doing in the bogs and watercourses of prehistoric Two Mile Borris, see [Prehistoric Two Mile Borris](../heritage/prehistoric.md). The two reports are on the Digital Repository of Ireland: [AR 35 / E2378](https://repository.dri.ie/catalog/8c980530t) and [AR 37 / E2379](https://repository.dri.ie/catalog/8k721x98k).
