A Bronze Age burnt mound site excavated by Gary Conboy in November 2006, ahead of the M8 build. The excavation produced two sub-sites about thirty metres apart on the same low rise.

A burnt mound, "fulacht fia" in Irish, is one of the most common prehistoric site types in the Irish landscape. The recipe is simple: a sunken wooden trough, a fireplace beside it to heat large stones red-hot, and a low horseshoe-shaped mound of cracked, fire-shattered stones that builds up next to the trough over years of repeated use. What people were doing in the troughs is still debated: cooking, brewing, washing wool, dyeing, and ritual bathing have all been proposed. Whatever they were, they were popular, and you find them everywhere in Ireland from roughly 2500 BC to 1000 BC.

The earlier of the AR 35 sub-sites (Site B) had two troughs and five pits. One trough produced a radiocarbon date of 2486-2299 cal BC, right at the late Neolithic to Early Bronze Age transition. The later sub-site (Site A) was a classic Middle Bronze Age burnt mound with a single irregular trough underneath; timbers from the trough dated to 1880-1660 cal BC.

The neighbouring sites along the same M8 stretch include [AR 36](dig_ar36.md), where an Early Bronze Age cremation cemetery and an early medieval iron-smithing phase shared the same field a few centuries apart, and [AR 37](dig_ar37.md), a second burnt mound site nearby. The map position for AR 35 is our conversion of the published Irish National Grid reference and may need a small adjustment when we confirm the coordinates with TII. For more on the prehistoric story see [Prehistoric Two Mile Borris](../heritage/prehistoric.md). The full report is on the [Digital Repository of Ireland](https://repository.dri.ie/catalog/8c980530t).
