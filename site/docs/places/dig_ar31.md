This is the biggest of the seven M8 archaeological sites along the N75 link road south of the village. The dig ran 300 metres east to west, straddled the Black River, and crossed the line between Borris townland on the east bank and Blackcastle townland on the west. Excavation took twelve months in 2006 and 2007, directed by Mícheál Ó Droma for Valerie J. Keeley Ltd. The site produced five distinct periods of activity across roughly 3,600 years.

On the east bank, on a small ridge in what is now arable field, the dig found a Middle Bronze Age roundhouse 6.8 metres in diameter, with an annex or porch on the east side, dating to the 15th to 17th centuries BC. A single Late Bronze Age cremation pit was also recovered, containing the burnt remains of an adolescent dated 1265-1060 BC. Then a long gap, before the same patch of ground was put to use again at the beginning of the 12th century AD.

The medieval activity is the headline. Two vertical watermills sat on the Black River, a 12th century one to the north and a 13th century one to the south. The southern mill's wheel-pit still held large oak timbers when the dig opened, including Timber 5, more than five metres long, with its lap joints and dowels intact. The "Borris Master Chronology", a new Irish dendrochronology run, comes from these timbers and spans 898 AD to 1176 AD; the trees were felled in the early 13th century. Just east of the mill, three corn-drying kilns of "dumbbell" type processed the grain; a metal-working complex with iron and copper smithing hearths sat further upslope; and a small earth-walled rectangular workshop nearby went through four superimposed floor levels before ending its life as a smithy. In a pit near the workshop the diggers found 57 silver pennies in a single hoard, mostly Edward I and Edward II English pennies plus an Irish Dublin penny and a Flemish coin of Valeran of Ligny. A unique decorated bone handle, carved from a horse metacarpal in the shape of a castle, sat in the same pit. The Bruce invasion of 1315-1318 is one plausible reason somebody buried that much and never came back for it.

On the west bank, in Blackcastle townland and right under the shadow of the Black Castle tower house, the dig found a 5.8 by 6 metre earth-walled medieval building with four successive floors, ending as a smithy with hammerscale in its hearth. Two adjacent stone-built circular post-medieval buildings, probably stores, sat nearby (16th to 17th century). And in the fields a little further west, a cluster of early modern limekilns marked 19th century commercial lime-burning.
