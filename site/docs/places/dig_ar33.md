This was the densest and longest-lived of the M8 dig sites, on an east-facing slope overlooking the Black River. Excavation ran from September 2006 to October 2007 and unearthed seven distinct phases of occupation, from a Middle Bronze Age burnt-pit complex right through to post-medieval field boundaries. AR 33 is where most of what we now know about prehistoric ritual and early medieval settlement in Two Mile Borris actually comes from.

The earliest big feature was a Late Bronze Age oval enclosure (Enclosure D), 95 metres east to west, with a 3.8 metre wide southeast entrance flanked by two large postholes. Inside the enclosure, on the raised central area, the diggers found the unforgettable cattle burial: the carefully arranged bones of a mature cow, around twelve years old, placed in three or four overlapping crisscrossed layers in a small pit, with three small wooden stakes driven into the ground first. Cattle that old in the Bronze Age were almost certainly not just farm animals. A fragment of human skull also came out of the ditch at the northern terminus, alongside in-situ burning, a flint scraper, and 90 kg of animal bone.

After the enclosure went out of use the ditch silted up and a smaller "plectrum"-shaped stockaded enclosure (Enclosure B) was built on the site, dated to the 5th to 8th centuries. It had a defended entrance and an internal palisade. Inside, a circular timber house, twenty-seven pits used as hearths, storage and roasting, a red deer antler handle, a ceramic crucible, and iron-working residues.

Then came the ringfort (Enclosure A), a classic 7th to 12th century round earthwork built over the northern half of the plectrum. Its ditch was V-shaped in profile and the east-facing entrance was 3.2 metres wide. Inside, four circular structures, one of them 5.2 metres in diameter with a half-cobbled floor that matches a textbook description of an Irish farmer's house from the early medieval law-tracts. The finds from the ringfort ditch include iron knives, a fragment of a quern-stone, a glass bead, a stone gaming-board, a copper-alloy ringed pin, and 26 kg of animal bone.

A larger rectangular enclosure (Enclosure C) followed in the same place, using the old ringfort as one corner and extending the working perimeter to around 87 by 55 metres. Inside were an iron furnace, a smithing hearth, two cereal-drying kilns, a probable granary, and two more roundhouses.

Just outside the enclosures, on the line of the original Enclosure D ditch, the diggers uncovered an 11th century inhumation cemetery: eighteen east-west burials in shallow unlined graves, with two further isolated burials nearby. One adult woman had been buried with a copper-alloy ring-pin tucked beneath the vertebrae of her neck.

The story of the people who lived in those enclosures, and who probably walked their dead up the road to Liathmore once Christian burial took over, is on [Early Medieval Two Mile Borris](../heritage/early-medieval.md). The cattle burial and the wider prehistoric picture are on [Prehistoric Two Mile Borris](../heritage/prehistoric.md).
